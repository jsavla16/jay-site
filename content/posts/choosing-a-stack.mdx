Before there was a single line of code, there was Canva. That's where I built the homepage and the about us page: getting the theme right, the colours, the fonts, the vintage feel that runs through everything Umojah does. I brought in the elements that make Umojah what it is, the Kenyan and East African foundation underneath a reggae sound system: Maasai warriors, the Maasai necklace as a medallion, and a rendering of the Umojah speaker stack itself.

Before that design work, the only presence Umojah had online was our social media pages and a listing on soundsystem.world. That was it. We needed to tell people that we had other services, that we hired out equipment, that a record label was coming, and that we were legitimate. Canva was where that started.

The speaker stack image took the longest, and it's worth telling honestly because it's the first place an AI tool actually got something wrong on this project. I used ChatGPT to try to get the exact likeness of Umojah's stack. It nailed the feel every time: the vintage sketch quality, the grainy poster texture. What it kept getting wrong were the technical details, the dimensions, the breather cavities, the parts that only mean something if you've actually built one of these boxes with your hands. I burned through a lot of credit trying to correct it. And I learned something about working with these tools that had nothing to do with speaker design: staying in the same chat past a certain point gave diminishing returns. Once that happened, starting a new chat and re-explaining what I wanted usually got me further than pushing on in the one that was already going stale.

Here's the thing though: Canva never really broke down. It did exactly what I asked of it, and I knew going in that all it could give me was design. What I didn't fully reckon with, and this is a separate confession from the Canva one, is that I built that design entirely for desktop. I knew, somewhere at the back of my head, that most people would see this on a phone. I designed for the big screen anyway. That mistake gets its own post later in this series, because what it did to the actual site was worse than anything Canva's limitations caused. For now it's enough to say: I had tunnel vision, and design software had nothing to do with it.

So if Canva wasn't the problem, what was? Canva is a design tool. It was never going to give me a CMS to manage upcoming events, new music releases, and collaborations as they happened. It wasn't going to give me SEO infrastructure, or a way to scale past a handful of static pages. Those were things I needed the moment I decided Umojah needed to look like a real business, not just a beautiful poster.

Working that out, and choosing what to replace Canva with, took about a week. I didn't do it alone. I talked to industry experts, mostly designers who weren't website builders themselves, and asked them where they'd go if they didn't want to pay a developer. The platforms that came up most in those conversations were Lovable, Squarespace, and Wix, each discussed in terms of what a designer, someone like me, could actually do on them without writing code.

Alongside those conversations, I put the same question to an AI assistant, and asked it to score the real contenders against the things that actually mattered for this project: design freedom, ease of use, CMS capability, SEO, performance, scalability, cost, and how ready each option was for the AI-driven workflows I wanted to build later. WordPress came out at 9.5 out of 10, on the strength of its CMS and its plugin ecosystem, but with real maintenance and security overhead attached. Squarespace scored 8, easy and polished but limited the moment Umojah's content (music, artists, events, collections) started to grow. Wix landed at 7.5, easy to use but historically weak on SEO, with a warning that large sites become difficult to manage on it. Framer scored 8.5, the best pure design freedom of any option, but a CMS that was, in its own words, "still relatively simple," better suited to blogs and case studies than a music catalogue and a growing event archive. Lovable also scored 8.5, an AI-first builder that generates a working React application from a description, strong for fast iteration but not really a CMS on its own.

Then there was the option of just building it in code: React, Next.js, Tailwind, a headless CMS like Sanity, Vercel for hosting. The AI assistant scored that one an 11 out of 10. Its reasoning, verbatim: "Yes... I'm giving it 11. Because it removes almost every limitation." Realistic costs across all of these sat in a similar band. WordPress running roughly £150 to £300 a year once hosting, a theme, and plugins were accounted for. Squarespace around £20 to £30 a month. Wix £10 to £25. Framer £15 to £30. Lovable free to start, with paid plans from around $20 a month. The code route, once you're past the free tiers of Vercel, Sanity, and a domain, realistically landed at £10 to £30 a month too. Cost wasn't actually what separated the options. Capability was.

What changed the comparison from theoretical to specific was showing the AI assistant the Canva design itself. Once it had actually seen the artboard, its recommendation got stronger, not weaker. Its read was that this wasn't a traditional content-heavy CMS site at all. It was a highly designed, editorial, largely static experience: roughly seventy percent of the page (hero, story, the sound system, services, general branding) barely changes from month to month. Another twenty percent (upcoming events, music releases, collaborations, merchandise, crew bios) updates every few weeks. Only about ten percent, new releases and social embeds, needed to feel genuinely live. That meant the CMS didn't need to run the whole site. It only needed to manage a handful of collections.

With that framing, the ranking shifted: Next.js with Sanity rose to a 10 out of 10, Framer to 9.2, WordPress and Lovable with Sanity tied at 8.5, Squarespace fell to 7, Wix to 6.5. Framer's jump made sense on paper. It's arguably the easiest way to build the kind of sectioned, animated, scroll-driven page the Canva design called for, but the same weakness kept showing up: the moment you add real dynamic content (events, music, artists, releases, collaborations) Framer's CMS starts to feel thin. Next.js paired with Sanity solved that by turning the update process into editing structured content (a title, date, venue, and poster for an event, say) rather than editing a page directly. The site would just populate itself from there.

There was one piece of pushback in that conversation I didn't expect, and it wasn't about the visual style. The AI assistant was clear it liked the retro editorial direction. What it flagged instead was the information architecture: everything on the Canva design was stacked into one very long page. Impressive on a first visit, it said, but harder to navigate as the content grows, and worse for SEO than giving each section (Home, About, Events, Music, Collaborations, Hire Us, Shop, Contact) its own dedicated page and URL. That single piece of feedback changed the shape of the whole site more than anything else in this decision.

None of that made the call for me, though. It was still mine to make, and it came down to what I actually had going for me. I'm a Python developer by background, focused on data science and machine learning, with a few online courses and the fastai library behind me. That's Python-heavy work, not website building. But I'd also dabbled in some consultancy work that used React, so the idea of Next.js wasn't a total leap into the dark. Add a design too bespoke for a drag-and-drop builder to reproduce faithfully, and a budget that was close to nothing, and Next.js with a headless CMS like Sanity was the only option that actually fit all three constraints at once.

So that's what "Canva wasn't enough" actually meant. Not that Canva failed at anything I asked it to do, but that a sound system trying to look like a legitimate, hireable business needed more than a beautiful static design could give it on its own. Canva got the look right. Getting Umojah a website that could grow with it meant leaving Canva behind and picking up code I didn't know how to write yet.
