In February 2016 I was standing on a beach in Goa in front of a sound system called 10,000 Lions, and something happened that I still don't have the words for that experience: How the vibrations went right through me, resonated with every cell in my body and shook me to my very core. The words finally had meaning and I remember thinking that there was no going back. Sound System found a hold in my life and 10 years later here we are.

That experience, at least for me, was when Umojah Sound System was conceived. There was no name yet, but it had been manifested. The understanding of what a sound system is finally clicked. The beginning had already started a few years earlier, without me knowing it, in conversations with my friend Joe.

Joe and I go back to the early 2010s. He'd already moved past being a casual reggae fan by then. He'd spent the 2000s at university in Nottingham, and somewhere in that decade he went from box boy to system engineer at Highness Sound System. He'd talk about it incessantly: the culture, the engineering, the way a properly built rig behaves in a room. I started listening to more dub and roots because of those conversations, years before I stood in front of a stack myself. When I finally did, in Goa, I understood what he'd been talking about the whole time.

I wasn't in Goa alone. Chirag, another founding member of Umojah, was there with me, and from the beach we went on to Dubai together and bought our first few components. A few days after we landed back in Kenya, the equipment we'd air-freighted arrived. When it arrived at his doorstep it was a total surprise for Joe as he wasn't expecting it at all. His excitement did the rest. Within three weeks we'd built our first two bass bins, mid-bass reflex boxes, and top boxes. In April 2016, we held our first session.

That's ten years of runway between "Joe talking about Nottingham" and where Umojah is now, and it's worth naming the people who did the actual building, because none of what follows in this series (a website, a stack, a decision to build it myself) makes sense without them.

Joe is Umojah's engineer and, honestly, the reason the sound exists at all. He designed the bassline that people now describe, unprompted, as one of the things that makes Umojah what it is. It's earned a reputation past Kenya's borders. Sheel, who goes by Dread Steppa, is the crew's musical pin: production skills, an ear for what a dub session needs, and five years of work he's finally about to release. And more recently, Danson has joined as the person who's done the actual research into hitting specific frequencies correctly, his knowledge has been central to the new box designs we're running now. Under all of that is a support crew of box boys who do the physical work of getting a stack built, run, and torn down.

What Umojah actually does, at its core, is promote sound system culture in Kenya. That's the whole mission, plainly stated: the messages that come out of roots, reggae and dub, and just as much, awareness of how and why sound systems like ours get built in the first place. We've run residencies at clubs, locked down stages at regional festivals, and since May 2023 we've run our own night, Nairobi Dub Club, in Westlands, the commercial and nightlife centre of Nairobi. We're 18 sessions deep as of writing. It happens roughly once every two months, always a Saturday, and it draws a genuinely mixed crowd: people from multicultural backgrounds out for a night, and hardcore reggae and dub enthusiasts who know exactly what they came for.

On paper, a Nairobi Dub Club night runs from 9pm to 6am. For the crew, it's longer than that. We start setting up the stack at 4pm, on the sixth level of a multistorey parking structure, and by the time everything's broken down and loaded out, it's 10am the next morning. My smartwatch logged 20,000 steps on the last one.

The mission, promoting the culture and not just throwing parties, is also why the boxes themselves matter as much as the music. Sound system culture is as much about the build as the playback: the retro look of properly built wooden speakers, the engineering behind why they sound the way they do. More people have started noticing that recently, and recognising that it's not just aesthetic: the boxes perform. That's led to something we didn't originally set out to do: people asking to hire the equipment. We've done a handful of non-reggae gigs on the back of that. The most recent was an RnB and house night. It sounded fantastic, and it told me something: that Umojah's sound has a use outside the culture we built it for.

"Umojah" itself is a deliberate spelling of the Kiswahili word *umoja*: unity. That's not a branding flourish; it's the actual reason the sound exists, and it's the reason "promoting sound system culture" isn't just about playing music people like. A properly run session is one of the few spaces where a genuinely mixed Saturday-night crowd in Westlands ends up standing in the same room, moved by the same bassline.

Which brings me to why, in 2026, after ten years of building this thing by hand, we needed a website.

The honest answer is commercial. Umojah is trying to become more sustainable, and that means a few things happening at once: renting out the equipment to people who've heard about the wooden boxes and want that sound at their own event; Umojah Records, which is close to releasing music after years of Sheel working on it quietly; and merchandise, which we sell but have no real way to show anyone outside of Instagram. None of that has anywhere to live right now except a modest social media following and word of mouth.

There's a less tangible reason too. A website is a credibility signal in a way a social media page isn't, especially for anyone deciding whether to hire equipment from us or trust us with an event. Followers don't tell a promoter or a client anything about whether you're serious. A working site, with real information on it, does.

So that's the why: a friendship that started before I understood what it was building toward, a beach in Goa that made it real, a crew that's grown from three people building bass bins in three weeks to something closer to an institution in Nairobi's nightlife, and, ten years in, a genuinely practical need for a website that could carry the hire business, the record label, and the merchandise credibly.

What I didn't have was a developer, a budget for one, or any background in building software. What I did have was an AI assistant, more stubbornness than sense, and, as it turned out, a fairly hard lesson in what "we tried Canva first" actually meant. That's the next post.
