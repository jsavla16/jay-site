SEO was never an afterthought on this project. It was always part of the plan, I'd just deliberately left it in the pipeline for after the design and build work, because the whole point of a website is traffic, and there's no point optimising a page that doesn't exist yet. By the tail end of the build, with most of the site already done, that moved from "later" to "now."

The research itself started with something almost embarrassingly simple: searching for businesses similar to ours and paying attention to how the results shifted when I changed the phrasing slightly. "Sound system hire Nairobi" pulls up a different picture than "sound system for hire near me," which is different again from "how much does it cost to hire a sound system." None of that needs a paid tool. Ten minutes in an incognito browser window, set to google.co.ke, typing a phrase and reading the autocomplete dropdown without pressing enter, tells you more about what people in this specific market actually type than guessing ever would. That same rabbit hole is also what got me thinking properly about Google's ranking system in the first place, and led me to register the business on Google Search Console, which is its own story for a later post.

Some of what came back from that dropdown wasn't something I'd have thought to write a page for at all. Alongside the obvious searches sat things like "is it cheaper to rent or buy a sound system," "how much does it cost to hire a sound tech," and "dj equipment hire price list." None of those had occurred to me as things people actually type before I went looking. That's the real value in doing this research yourself rather than skipping straight to building: the searches you didn't expect are the ones nobody has written a page for yet, which makes them worth more than the obvious ones everyone is already fighting over.

Sorting the realistic targets from the hopeless ones mattered just as much as finding them. "Reggae sound system hire Nairobi," "dub sound system Kenya," "Allen and Heath Xone:92 hire Nairobi," a Technics SL1210 Mark II turntable for hire, these are specific and low competition enough that Umojah can actually win them, because nobody else searching those terms is looking for a generic AV company. Plain "amplifier hire" or "speaker hire" are the opposite: generic, high competition, and not worth a dedicated page when six other companies are already fighting over the exact same words.

The competitor that mattered most was soundhirenairobi.co.ke. Their approach is a page for every single search term, at real scale, and their footer alone links out to things like "Pioneer Cdj 2000 Rental" and "Sound System For Church Worship." More useful than any individual page was the way they'd segmented the whole market: by industry (corporate, weddings, religious, political campaigns, education, hospitality, sports, film) and by event type (conferences, rallies, crusades, product launches, gala nights). That taxonomy is a map of how the market actually thinks about this service. It's not how I would have organised the site if I'd been left to my own instincts, which would have started from what Umojah is rather than what someone typing into Google is actually looking for.

The same research also showed where they were weak, and the weaknesses were as instructive as the strategy. Their meta description reads "Premium Our Comprehensive Services Industries We Serve in Nairobi Kenya," machine assembled, the kind of line that tells you nobody actually wrote it. Their footer also carries what's plainly a link farm: gold and gemstone exporters, safari operators, visa processing, motorcycle spares, web hosting, businesses that have nothing to do with sound equipment and exist purely to trade links. Google has gotten better at discounting exactly that kind of thing, so it's a long term liability for them, not an asset. And for all their scale, they're one of six near identical general purpose AV hire outfits with nothing to remember them by. Umojah is the only reggae and dub sound system in Kenya, hand built, with a decade of festivals behind it. Nobody else can write that page. Competing on "PA hire Nairobi" against six generic competitors is a losing fight. Competing on identity plus specific named gear is winnable. That distinction genuinely changed how I started thinking about laying out our own pages, organising around what people are actually searching for rather than around how the business sees itself internally.

The pricing question needed a different kind of research, the kind you only get from actually doing business here rather than reading about it. Kenya runs on bargaining. Fruit, a TV, a sound system for hire, it's all the same instinct, and publishing a fixed rate on the website was never really an option once I thought about it properly. The sharper reason underneath that isn't just cultural habit, though. A published rate card removes your ability to price a festival organiser differently from someone hiring gear for a birthday party, and that's one of the oldest tricks available to a business like ours, one that only works as long as the number never gets printed anywhere public. One competitor, eventsandpartyrentals.co.ke, does put a floor figure on their site, "from KSh 20,000." That doesn't really undercut the point. A floor isn't a rate card. You can publish a floor and still charge whatever the room in front of you can bear above it.

I want to be honest about where this actually stands right now, because this post is research and decisions, not a finished feature. The plan is a page that answers "how much does it cost to hire a sound system" not with a number, but with what actually determines the price, event size, distance, equipment needed, that kind of thing. That page doesn't exist yet. Neither does the Hire page itself. The research had already suggested splitting Hire and Custom Build into separate pages, since they're different buyers with different budgets and different searches entirely. I've taken that further than the research suggested: Custom Build is getting spun off into its own separate site with its own brand and identity, not just its own page on this one. That's a bigger decision than an SEO recommendation usually produces, and it came directly out of sitting with this research rather than just implementing what it said.

One other recommendation is worth naming because it costs nothing and isn't built yet either: a Google Business Profile. It's free, and for "near me" and map searches it outranks everything on the website itself, no matter how well the site is optimised. It's probably the single highest return for the least effort on the whole list, which is exactly why it's next.

So that's the research and the reasoning. None of it involved a paid tool, an agency, or anything I couldn't have done on a laptop with a free afternoon. What it did require was actually sitting with the results instead of skimming them, and being willing to let a competitor's weaknesses, and Kenya's own buying habits, change decisions I'd otherwise have made on instinct. What actually gets built from it, the pages, the metadata, the Search Console saga I mentioned earlier, is next.
