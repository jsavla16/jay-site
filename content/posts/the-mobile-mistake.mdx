A week and a half into the build, I opened the site on my own phone, a OnePlus 12R, expecting to see some rough edges. What I actually saw was weird enough that I sat with it for a second before I understood what I was looking at. It wasn't a smaller version of the site. It was the exact desktop layout, wide sections and all, just crammed into a strip the width of my hand. Nothing had stacked. Nothing had resized. Text I could read comfortably on my monitor was, on the phone, unreadable.

I'm used to my phone rendering websites badly sometimes, a menu that doesn't quite collapse, an image that overflows. This wasn't that. This was the entire page, top to bottom, still laid out as if it had a 1920px monitor to work with, just squeezed down until it fit a 390px screen by force. Nothing had reflowed. It had just shrunk, the way a photo shrinks, and text shrinks along with everything else in a photo.

I want to be honest about what kind of mistake this actually was, because "I forgot about mobile" isn't quite it. Mobile had always been part of the plan. I knew there was a separate round of design work still owed to it, a dedicated pass in Canva before any mobile build started, and the decision to solve the desktop version first was a deliberate one, not an oversight. What I got wrong was assuming that gap was safe to leave open for a while. I hadn't reckoned with what the site would actually do on a phone in the meantime, and I only found out by opening it on one.

What I was looking at technically comes straight from the system I wrote about last post. Every length on the site is a fraction of one shared value, `--stage`. On a desktop monitor, that value is driven by a wide window, so a fraction like 0.0259 turns into a perfectly normal heading size. On a phone, `--stage` gets capped by the viewport width instead, which on a 390px-wide screen is tiny to begin with. Every fraction of it collapses toward the same tiny number along with it. Body copy computed to 5.3px. Buttons came out 10.8px tall with 5.2px text, roughly a quarter of the 44px minimum you need for something to be comfortably tappable with a thumb. Not "a bit small." Genuinely unusable, on the device most people in Kenya would actually be using to look at this.

That last part is what turned this from an embarrassing bug into something worth stopping everything else for. Kenya's web traffic is overwhelmingly mobile. A site that looked genuinely good on the machine it was built on was, at the same time, illegible to most of the people it actually existed for. Both of those things were true at once, and only one of them was visible from where I'd been sitting.

The Hero was the clearest example. On desktop, two Maasai warriors flank the medallion, standing either side of it like a guard, with the call-to-action buttons sitting cleanly below. On the phone, that whole composition just got squeezed into a narrow column: the warriors overlapping the medallion, and the CTA buttons rendering on top of the warriors. Everything that was meant to be a deliberate arrangement had turned into a pile.

Fixing it wasn't a matter of telling the AI to "make it responsive" and letting it figure out the rest. The first step happened back in Canva, not in code: a proper second design pass, section by section, built specifically at mobile size rather than shrunk from the desktop one. Only once that existed did the AI come back in, working from screenshots of the new mobile designs to figure out the `--stage` adjustments needed to match them.

That order mattered, and it's the part of this I actually want to make explicit. The AI could size things correctly once I'd shown it what the mobile version should look like. What it couldn't do, looking at the desktop design alone, was decide that the warriors shouldn't be there at all on a phone. Dropping them was a human call, mine, not something the AI proposed or could have proposed from the inputs it had. On desktop those two figures do real work: they frame the medallion and read as protection around it. At phone width, with everything already tight for space, they stopped doing that job and started just being two extra shapes fighting the CTA for room. Once I'd made that call in Canva and handed over the new design, the AI's part was mechanical: work out the numbers that would make the phone layout match what I'd actually drawn.

That division held for every section I redesigned, not just the Hero. The AI was very good at the part that comes after a decision: measuring, calculating, writing the fraction that makes a redrawn layout hold together at any width. It had nothing to offer on the part that comes before a decision, what actually belongs on a small screen and what's just clutter once the space that justified it is gone. That's not a criticism of the tool. It's just where the line sits, and this was the project's clearest lesson in exactly where.

The whole fix took a few days, not the full rebuild the size of the problem might suggest. Redesign a section in Canva, hand over the new mobile screens, let the AI translate that into `--stage` adjustments for that section, move to the next one. Repeatable, once the pattern was set.

Every section ended up with its own separate mobile version, in normal document flow rather than the absolutely-positioned desktop stage, so copy could push content down the page instead of being painted over by it. The Crew section, three columns side by side on desktop, became three stacked entries on mobile, left-aligned instead of centred, with the terracotta ground splitting so the header keeps its colour and the three bios sit on plain bone underneath. Services did the same in reverse: the two panels that are bone and terracotta on desktop invert their colours on mobile, and the audience tags, a fixed-width pill on desktop so the longest label wouldn't blow out the layout, just wrap onto a second line instead once there's no fixed panel width to protect. Small decisions, repeated section by section, and none of them were "shrink the desktop version." Each one was closer to redrawing the section with the same content and a different set of constraints.

What stuck with me from this one wasn't the pixel measurements, it was how confidently wrong the site looked before I actually checked. Every section had been reviewed, on the monitor it was built on, and looked finished. None of that reviewing would ever have caught this, because the thing that was broken only existed at a width I hadn't looked at. Mobile isn't the desktop layout scaled down. It's a different arrangement of the same parts, and the things that are load-bearing at 1920px, like two warriors framing a medallion, can just be noise at 360px. You only find that out by looking at 360px.
